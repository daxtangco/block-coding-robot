Open: we built a low-cost, 3D-printed 5-DOF robotic arm for secondary-school robotics education. Students program it by dragging blocks, it sees objects with a camera, and sorts them. Guiding idea: the camera SEES, the PC THINKS, the arm ACTS.

In one sentence

The camera sees, the PC thinks, and the arm acts.

SEES ESP32-CAM (or laptop webcam) captures a frame
→
THINKS YOLOv8n on the PC detects & classifies the piece
→
ACTS ESP32 arm moves over WebSocket to sort it

Each job runs where it's best suited.

Why split it? The ESP32 is too weak for a neural net; the laptop can't ride a moving arm. Vision on the PC, motion on the microcontroller, talking over the arm's own WiFi.

The arm is its own WiFi hotspot — laptop and camera both join it. No home WiFi, no cloud.

Three tiers: (1) Laptop — browser Blockly IDE + FastAPI at localhost:8000 runs YOLOv8 inference, training, and firmware build/flash. (2) ESP32 arm controller — WiFi access point, serves its control page, drives 5 servos via PCA9685 over WebSocket. (3) ESP32-CAM (optional) — streams JPEG frames, auto-joins the arm's network. The arm is the AP (SSID RobotArm-XXXX from its MAC). Laptop/phone AND camera join THAT network. 192.168.4.1 = arm; camera auto-takes 192.168.4.50. No home WiFi, no cloud — classroom-deployable.

Built from cheap, off-the-shelf parts

  • 5-DOF · 12 printed parts · Esun PLA+ @ 20% infill (open-source Thingiverse 3039476)
  • MG996R ×3 (base/shoulder/elbow) · MG90S ×2 (wrist/gripper)
  • PCA9685 16-ch PWM driver over I²C (SDA 21 / SCL 22)
  • Dedicated 5V/3A supply — servos isolated from the ESP32
Why a separate servo driver + supply? Offloading PWM to the PCA9685 frees the ESP32; isolating servo power on its own 5V/3A rail stopped current surges from resetting the board (see the optimization log). Per-joint travel caps {180,180,180,90,90} keep every servo in its mechanical range. Exact from Ch. 5/6: MG996R (metal-gear) for the 3 heavy joints, MG90S for wrist/gripper — do NOT say SG90, the paper specifies MG996R + MG90S.

How it's wired

[wiring-diagram.svg missing from docs/img/]

Walk it left-to-right: the ESP32 talks to the PCA9685 over I²C (GPIO21/22) plus 3.3V logic power. Servo power is a SEPARATE 5V/3A rail into the PCA9685's V+ terminal — never from the ESP32 — with all grounds tied together; that isolation is what stopped the brownout resets. Five servos hang off the PCA9685 on channels 0, 2, 4, 6, 8 (base, shoulder, elbow, wrist, gripper — the firmware's PWM_CHANNEL array). The ESP32-CAM has NO wires to the arm: it joins the arm's WiFi access point and sits at 192.168.4.50. If asked why channels are spaced 0/2/4/6/8: it's just the wiring we standardized on; the firmware maps logical joints to those physical channels.

How we doubled it — 5 fixes

ProblemFixOutcome
Servos buzz/jam into end-stops; MG996R overheats (~2.5 A stall) Narrowed PWM range 100–500 → 150–450 countsNo end-stop stalling; rail stopped sagging
All 5 servos start at once → ground bounce → board resets Move one joint per tick (serialized slewer, configurable order)Resets eliminated
Gripper stalls on object → starves shoulder Tuned CLOSE angle per piece width (10/13°, not 0°); travel cappedShoulder lifts freely; drove the 92.5% (SO3)
Servo spikes couple into ESP32 logic rail Separate 5V/3A servo supply via PCA9685 V+No further spurious resets
Browser blocks direct cam fetch (CORS) Backend /capture proxy (+ server-side rotate)Frames & boxes align, no CORS errors
This is Table 6.8 condensed — the "we actually engineered this" slide. IMPORTANT honesty: the gripper fix is an ANGLE + travel-cap strategy (set close angle so the servo reaches target instead of stalling), NOT electrical current sensing. There is no ADC on the servo line. If asked "how do you detect a stall?" — we don't measure current; we avoid commanding a stalling angle. The ~2.5 A figure is the MG996R's stall current we observed, not a live reading. The paper (SO3) states the 92.5% sorting rate "was driven by mechanical tuning of the gripper CLOSE angle and calibration of the pick/place poses" — so this row is the direct cause of the headline result. Close angles are per width: narrow 10° (1-stud), wide 13° (2-stud). The serialized slewer moves one joint per tick in a configurable order; note it is NOT soft-ramping (that's listed as future work) — it's fixed-step, staggered to kill the current surge.

Limitations & honest trade-offs

LimitationWhy / mitigation
Remaining failures are vision, not mechanicalCamera image quality + lighting; detection robustness is the main future target
Custom detection needs an annotated datasetTrain Model retrains YOLOv8n on a YOLOv8-format dataset (e.g. a Roboflow export) — not a few-shot "show it once"
Local-only control (no remote/internet)By design — offline classroom; AP range ~one room
Open-loop servos (no position feedback)Hobby servos; we avoid stall by capping travel & sizing grip, not by sensing current
95.6% mAP is on a clean validation setReal-world lighting is harder; two-stage training mitigates but doesn't erase the gap
Volunteering limitations builds credibility. The open-loop / no-current-sensing point is the one to be crisp on — it's the honest version of the "stall" story.

Future work

  • Improve detection robustness under classroom lighting (the remaining failure mode)
  • OTA firmware updates — drop the USB re-flash entirely
  • On-arm ESP32-CAM as the default vision source
  • Few-shot "show it once" teaching, so a custom class needs no annotated dataset
  • Larger classroom pilot & longitudinal usability study
Lead with detection robustness — the data (all 9 failures were vision) points straight there.
